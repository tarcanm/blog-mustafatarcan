<p><em>Strategic advantage comes from expanding what a supply chain can see, compare and learn from, not simply adding another AI tool.</em></p>
<h2>More choices, not more noise</h2>
<p>A port delay can leave a planning team debating the wrong question: which alternative route should we use? For a strategic review, the more useful question may be whether that route belongs in the network at all. Imagine a planner facing repeated exposure through one gateway. Switching lanes is one option. Changing supplier concentration, reserving capacity elsewhere or repositioning inventory should also be on the table before anyone starts ranking proposals.</p>

<p>This is where AI can improve supply chain strategy: not by selecting an answer, but by changing how alternatives are found, represented and challenged. Those three activities form the decision architecture. A team that examines only familiar options cannot select an unfamiliar alternative, however carefully it scores the shortlist. AI can help widen that shortlist, provided the search begins with verified requirements rather than an invitation to produce plausible suggestions.</p>
<p>A cross-industry example makes the distinction concrete. A software company used AI scouting against a database of more than 40 million public and private companies. In under a day, it identified and scored more than 500 potential acquisition targets. The team prioritized 15 leads and completed three acquisitions within months. This was acquisition search, not supply chain planning. Its relevance is the breadth of alternatives considered before people narrowed the field.</p>
<p>For sourcing or capacity decisions, that means asking for distinct configurations, not a longer list of nearly identical suppliers. Define qualification requirements, capacity limits, service commitments and financial boundaries first. Then ask AI to generate and screen alternatives against them, separating confirmed facts from assumptions. The planner&#8217;s next question becomes more useful: which options deserve investigation, and what evidence would justify rejecting them? Search breadth matters only when the screening remains disciplined.</p>
<h2>A living view of the network</h2>
<p>A broader choice set still needs a credible picture of the business. Unilever Ice Cream describes a network of 35 factories and about three million freezers across 60 countries, with 100,000 freezers enabled with AI. The company reported that weather data improved forecast accuracy by 10% in Sweden. It also reported that data from AI-enabled freezers increased retail orders and sales by 8% in Turkey, 12% in the US and up to 30% in Denmark.</p>

<p>Those are company-reported results, not proof that another network would achieve the same gains. For strategic planning, they illustrate why the representation matters. A capacity proposal based on factory output alone leaves different questions unanswered from one that also considers demand signals closer to customers. Before comparing scenarios, planners should ask what their model can actually see, which relationships it captures and where its information stops.</p>
<p>MYbank, part of Ant Group, offers another perspective. Its Goose System uses AI-powered knowledge graphs to map smaller businesses across supply chains and assess business status and creditworthiness. Ant and MYbank reported serving over one million automotive SMEs, with 64% receiving collateral-free loans for the first time. They reported that the loans enabled more orders, alongside a 17% increase in average monthly delivery volume. These are their reported results, not independently established conclusions here.</p>
<p>The strategic lesson is not that a planner should adopt a credit model. It is to question the boundaries of the network being represented. Does a sourcing comparison include the smaller businesses behind a direct supplier? Can the team trace a capacity assumption to its origin and check when it was updated? A richer model should expose missing information rather than disguise it. Coverage, lineage and freshness belong beside cost and service in the review.</p>
<h2>Make disagreement part of the process</h2>
<p>Now imagine the planner presenting the expanded shortlist. Instead of defending the first promising option, the planner asks colleagues to identify what would make each proposal fail. AI can support that discussion by proposing configurations, criticizing assumptions and examining how a competitor might respond. Keep those perspectives separate before combining them. The aim is structured disagreement, not a synthetic consensus that merely repeats the preferences of the most senior person present.</p>

<p>Evidence from P&amp;G offers a useful, bounded comparison. In a field experiment, 776 commercial and R&amp;D professionals worked on real product innovation tasks. Individuals using AI matched the average quality of two-person teams without AI, while AI teams were about 12% faster. AI also helped produce more balanced solutions across functions. This was not a supply chain planning experiment. It suggests a reason to test AI in cross-functional deliberation, not assume an equivalent planning result.</p>
<p>The harder turn is organizational: buying access to a model does not supply this decision architecture. Durable advantage rests on proprietary data, workflows, integration and the ability to learn from decisions. AI can be wrong, inconsistent or fabricate information. A challenge that sounds convincing still needs evidence. People remain accountable for approving a sourcing strategy, committing capacity or changing inventory allocation, including deciding when the available evidence is too weak to proceed.</p>
<p>On Monday, take three steps. First, choose one strategic decision and define what success requires. Second, supply verified data and explicit constraints, then have AI generate alternatives and criticize each against the same criteria. Third, have leaders select the course, record the reasoning and revisit assumptions against outcomes. The deliverable is not another recommendation deck. It is a defensible choice set and a record the organization can learn from.</p>
