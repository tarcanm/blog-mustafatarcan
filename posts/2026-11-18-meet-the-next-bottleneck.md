<p>The capacity project succeeds, and the delivery problem survives. In an illustrative factory network, a supplier raises output by 20%, yet the customer receives only 10% more finished product. Nobody has misreported the machine&#8217;s improvement. The investment has exposed the next limit, and the shipment promise assumed that limit did not exist.</p>
<p>Judge capacity investments by customer shipments after the next constraint takes over. A business case should explain how much additional saleable output reaches the customer after the upgrade, where that output stops growing, and what must happen before another increment becomes useful.</p>
<h2>The next limit sets the return</h2>
<p>Consider a deliberately simplified example. Three sequential operations can supply 100, 110 and 140 units per day. Each finished unit needs one unit from every operation. Assume steady operation, sufficient demand, reliable material supply and no scrap. The network can sustain 100 finished units per day because the first operation limits the flow.</p>

<p>Increase that operation&#8217;s capacity by 20%, to 120 units. Sustained finished output rises to 110, not 120, because the second operation now sets the ceiling. The local improvement is genuine. The network gain is 10%. Any payback calculation based on 20 additional daily shipments has credited the project with ten units it cannot yet deliver.</p>
<p>Raising the first operation to 150 instead would still leave sustained output at 110 under the same assumptions. The extra capacity might have another purpose, such as recovery after downtime or future demand growth. Those benefits need their own justification. They should not appear as immediate shipment gains while the next operation remains unchanged.</p>
<p>Real supply networks complicate this arithmetic. Product mix changes resource requirements, factories share equipment, yields vary and transport slots expire. Several constraints can bind together. A capacity ranking therefore needs a defined product family, planning period and operating assumptions. A long lead time can identify a candidate for investigation; it does not by itself prove which resource limits throughput.</p>
<h2>Prepare the next supplier early</h2>
<p>Return to the illustrative network and consider the planner responsible for customer promises. Sales expects the upgraded supplier to support 120 daily shipments. The planner can see that the second operation supports only 110. Accepting the larger commitment leaves someone explaining a preventable shortfall after the project has already been celebrated.</p>
<p>The planner brings the second operation&#8217;s owner into the review before commissioning. They establish usable capacity for the relevant mix, check the staffing assumptions and identify a possible improvement. The customer commitment stays at 110 until evidence supports more. This is a better outcome than discovering the same limitation through overdue orders and weekend recovery work.</p>

<p>Preparation need not mean funding every supplier at once. A nearby constraint may need a tooling trial, qualification work or a staffing decision before it needs equipment. Asking early preserves choices. Waiting until that supplier becomes the emergency can leave overtime or an expensive purchase as the only available response.</p>
<p>Suppliers also need enough context to prepare sensibly. Share the expected output after the current upgrade, the relevant product requirements and the date that capacity must become usable. Agree what each supplier will validate. A nominal machine rating offers little assurance if the required operators, approved materials or customer allocation are missing.</p>
<p>Do not turn this into a rule that all spending outside today&#8217;s bottleneck is waste. Safety, compliance and reliability can justify investment without an immediate throughput increase. Preparing tomorrow&#8217;s constraint can also be valuable. The discipline is to name the benefit honestly and avoid counting the same future shipments in several business cases.</p>
<h2>Fund the sequence, verify the flow</h2>
<p>For a throughput project, finance should evaluate the additional finished units that can actually be sold over a common horizon. Apply the appropriate contribution margin, then account for investment, extra operating costs and working capital. Include ramp time. An upgrade commissioned late in the period cannot earn a full period&#8217;s benefit.</p>
<p>Compare the sequence as well as the individual projects. In the example, upgrading the first operation and then raising the second to 125 would allow 120 units per day, with the first operation again limiting output. Evaluate that combined gain against the combined cost. Do not add two standalone forecasts that both claim the same ten extra units.</p>

<p>The sequence should remain conditional. A change in demand mix, an improvement that underperforms or a newly constrained shared resource can alter the next decision. Keep the second supplier ready without pretending the original ranking will stay correct indefinitely. Reassess the network when the first improvement produces measured results.</p>
<p>After commissioning, check completed customer shipments alongside local output. If the upgraded operation runs faster while work accumulates before the next stage, investigate release rates and the new constraint before buying more capacity at the old one. A better local utilization report cannot settle whether the network delivers more.</p>
<p>At the next capital review, ask the project owner to show today&#8217;s shipment ceiling and the expected ceiling after the upgrade. Name the resource that sets each limit, the evidence behind its usable capacity and the owner preparing the next step. Approve the investment against that shipment gain, then revisit the sequence when actual flow confirms or contradicts the plan.</p>
