<p>Imagine a transport system reporting lower freight costs while a pallet misses its departure. In this hypothetical, it cancels a confirmed booking to consolidate the load. The replacement carrier&#8217;s capacity record has expired, but the system treats the booking request as a commitment. The evening dispatcher discovers the mistake when the customer calls.</p>
<p>She can explain what happened. She cannot authorize the recovery premium or stop the same rule affecting tomorrow&#8217;s orders. Asking her to own the outcome without those permissions makes her the messenger for a decision somebody else delegated.</p>
<p>Autonomous decisions need a human owner before anything goes wrong. That means assigning responsibility for the customer response and for the policy that allowed the action. Operational ownership is different from legal liability, which depends on contracts and applicable law. IT investigates technical and data failures; the business owns the service tradeoff. Any vendor obligations need separate contractual review.</p>

<h2>Define exactly what the system may decide</h2>
<p>Start with one decision class, such as rebooking an outbound shipment. The transport owner approves its boundaries, names the responsible delegate on each shift, and assigns a backup. The record should say who may change the policy, who handles exceptions and who communicates a changed customer commitment. A department name alone cannot answer an urgent call.</p>
<p>Inside those boundaries, the system might rebook an approved carrier within agreed cost and service limits. Changing a customer promise requires separate authority. Safety restrictions, sanctions controls and contractual transport conditions remain binding. A cheaper booking cannot justify bypassing them, and an unknown account classification should trigger review rather than an assumption that ordinary terms apply.</p>
<p>Cost limits also need an aggregation rule. Several individually acceptable rebookings can consume a shift&#8217;s entire recovery budget. Decide how the system checks cumulative exposure across related actions, not just whether each transaction sits below its own cap. Otherwise, a rule intended to limit discretion can authorize a much larger commitment than anyone approved.</p>
<p>Write down which inputs must be current enough to support execution. In the opening scenario, carrier capacity needs a validity check before the confirmed booking is released. Missing or expired evidence should move the decision out of autonomous execution. These are proposed operating controls, consistent with the continuous risk management described in the <a href="https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">voluntary AI risk framework</a>, not universal thresholds supplied by a standard.</p>

<h2>Escalation needs a deadline and an answer</h2>
<p>An escalation is useful only while someone can still change the outcome. Set acknowledgement deadlines before the last feasible dispatch, allowing time for the backup to act. Triggers should include threatened customer promises, stale capacity and requests outside the agreed authority. The dispatcher needs the affected orders and practical alternatives, not an unexplained confidence score.</p>
<p>Silence must not count as approval. Agree the fallback beforehand: perhaps retain a confirmed booking while a replacement remains unconfirmed, or pause the affected decision class. Holding freight is not always safe, especially for time-sensitive cargo. The fallback must fit the load and available options rather than apply one blanket instruction to every exception.</p>
<p>The decision record should capture the input snapshot and timestamps, the options considered, the model and policy versions, the relevant limit and the authority used. Link these to the action request, carrier acknowledgement and actual execution outcome through one decision identifier. Record rejected options when they explain a consequential tradeoff.</p>
<p>A generated explanation does not prove that a carrier accepted a booking or collected a pallet. Keep those confirmations separately, with enough detail to reconcile what happened. Use access-controlled, append-only records and retention periods suited to contractual and other applicable requirements. Auditing operational evidence does not require collecting a model&#8217;s hidden chain of thought.</p>
<h2>Give people control over stopping and restarting</h2>
<p>The designated shift authority should be able to stop pending actions and return the affected decision class to advisory mode without waiting for a vendor ticket. That permission needs testing before an incident. It cannot reverse a departed truck or cancel a binding commitment automatically, so the recovery owner must check the actual execution state before authorizing another booking.</p>

<p>Record who intervened, when, why and which orders were affected. Reconcile cancellations and replacement confirmations against the original decision identifier to avoid paying for two trucks. Restart should be a separate permission held by the designated business owner, exercised after technical validation and a bounded rehearsal. A cleared error message alone is insufficient evidence that the operating problem has been fixed.</p>
<p>Return to the hypothetical dispatcher during that rehearsal. This time, the expired capacity record blocks the cancellation and reaches her before cutoff. She keeps the confirmed booking while the backup checks alternatives. That is a possible better outcome to test, not a claim that automation will never miss another shipment. The exercise exposes whether her permissions match the responsibility assigned to her.</p>
<p>Review late escalations, service breaches and the actual outcomes of overrides, rather than rewarding recommendation acceptance. Separate a reasonable intervention from a policy or data failure. Before expanding autonomy, replay one recent transport decision with its shift owner. Demonstrate the pause, follow the escalation to an acknowledgement, reconcile the execution evidence and require an explicit restart decision.</p>
