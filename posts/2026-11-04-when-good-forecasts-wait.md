<p>A forecast can be right and still arrive too late to help anyone. That becomes less surprising when the work is viewed from the last moment when a practical response is still possible. A demand signal may improve every morning while the supplier cutoff, production slot, or transport booking quietly moves beyond reach. The forecast may remain accurate even after its operational usefulness has narrowed.</p>
<p>The operational question is therefore narrower than whether the forecast was good. When did the signal become actionable, and how much decision slack remained before the last feasible action time? This is a proposed diagnostic, not a universal framework. It cannot create capacity, material, transport, or approval where none exists. It can, however, expose waiting that has been mistaken for prudence.</p>

<h2>Accuracy does not preserve an option</h2>
<p>Imagine, hypothetically, a distributor sees a sustained demand uplift for one product. The forecast becomes more credible as new orders arrive. The commercial team reviews it on Tuesday, inventory planners discuss it on Thursday, and the supplier order cutoff is Friday. If confirmation and execution take time, Thursday agreement may already be too late, even though the forecast remains directionally right.</p>
<p>That delay is easy to miss because each review can look reasonable in isolation. Someone checks the signal, another validates stock, and a colleague waits for commercial context. No single pause appears reckless, yet the sequence consumes the remaining option. By approval, the supplier may offer only a later slot, or none.</p>
<p>Better forecasts do not automatically produce better outcomes. Their value depends on reaching a decision while a viable action still exists.</p>
<p>This creates a difficult human consequence for the customer service colleague. They may have to explain a late delivery by saying that the business saw the increase but waited until the response could no longer affect supply. The colleague is left defending a process that preserved evidence and approvals while losing the customer&#8217;s practical promise.</p>
<h2>Define slack from the external cutoff</h2>
<p>A useful review starts by defining the last feasible decision time for the decision in question. That time is not simply the published supplier order cutoff. It should be calculated by working backward from that external cutoff, allowing time for prerequisite validation, internal approvals, supplier processing and confirmation, and the actual release of the order or schedule change. Later production, allocation, transport, and delivery milestones matter separately; the final delivery or production event does not need to finish before the supplier order cutoff.</p>

<p>Decision slack is the time remaining between the current moment and that back planned decision time. It changes as the signal, constraints, and confirmations change. A review held with comfortable slack may be deliberate monitoring. An owned decision can still be late if it is made after the last feasible decision time. A deadline and an owner do not restore an option that has already been lost.</p>
<p>Waiting is not always a defect. Forecast uncertainty may justify postponing a commitment, especially when false positives create obsolete inventory, expediting cost, customer confusion, or a compliance risk. Intentional monitoring is reasonable before the deadline when an owner, a reason, and a revisit trigger are explicit. The trigger might be a new order threshold, a supplier response, or a specified review time. Waiting without those controls leaves no reliable basis for knowing whether the choice is still being managed.</p>
<p>The diagnostic should record timestamps rather than opinions. Record when the signal was available and seen, when material and capacity constraints were checked, when supplier confirmation was requested, and when the order or schedule change was actually released. Compare each event with the last feasible decision time. The relevant question is not simply when the forecast was issued or when the final service result appeared, but whether each prerequisite still fit inside the remaining window.</p>
<h2>Trace the action into the physical world</h2>
<p>A planner may release a change while the supplier has not confirmed it, a production slot may remain provisional, or a transport booking may still be unavailable. Verification should follow the decision until the relevant physical or commercial commitment is real. Otherwise a dashboard can report rapid approval while the customer facing outcome remains unchanged.</p>
<p>In the hypothetical distributor example, an earlier supplier check would reveal uncertainty sooner, but it would not guarantee service. If the supplier permits it, the team might request a conditional slot, with its commercial obligations and cancellation or commitment costs understood before anyone accepts it. The team could also seek an approved substitute or deliberately wait with a visible deadline. No path demonstrates that actual service was achieved; it only preserves or clarifies the choices still available.</p>

<p>A practical test is to trace one recent late order through actual timestamps and cutoffs. Identify the first credible demand signal, the last feasible decision time, the durations needed for validation, approvals, supplier confirmation, and release, and the point at which each owner knew the remaining slack. Then locate where monitoring stopped being intentional or where a decision became late. If action was impossible before the signal was trusted, the issue may be forecast uncertainty or a structural constraint. If action remained possible but no one moved, the issue is decision latency.</p>
<p>This distinction avoids blaming the forecast for a missed option while also avoiding the comforting claim that accuracy alone should have saved the order. Measuring the distance to the last feasible decision time makes the constraint visible, including cases where no better response was physically available. The review should end with a recorded owner, the next trigger, and the exact release or supplier-confirmation step required for the remaining option.</p>
