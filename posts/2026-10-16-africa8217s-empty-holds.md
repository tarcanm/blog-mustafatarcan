<h2>The wrong kind of growth</h2>
<p>In August 2026, African airlines added 14 percent more cargo capacity while demand grew 3 percent. Their cargo holds were 36.5 percent full, the emptiest of any region on earth, against a global average of 46 percent. Capacity grew faster than in any other region, yet the planes carried less of what they could take. The arithmetic feels wrong because the investment story sounds right.</p>

<p><strong>Africa&#8217;s cargo bet is not won by adding capacity. It is won behind the border.</strong></p>
<p>The money is already committed, and it is not small. A global logistics group announced a multi year investment of more than 300 million euros across Sub-Saharan Africa in October 2025. The programme covers express, forwarding and contract logistics. It includes upgraded gateways, more aviation uplift and guaranteed transit coverage reaching second tier cities. It also targets energy and industrial projects, cold chain and pharmaceutical flows, plus temperature sensitive healthcare capability and transport led solutions, with expansion in South Africa.</p>
<p>Africa&#8217;s largest carrier has made an equally large bet. It ordered ten freighters, eight of one larger type and two of another, with a catalogue value of about 5 billion dollars, and took options on eight more. It already operates twelve freighters of the larger type, while its fleet exceeds 140 aircraft. At the same time, it is preparing an airport designed to handle 3.7 million tonnes of freight a year, at a cost of about 12.5 billion dollars.</p>
<h2>Capacity arrived first</h2>
<p>Capacity arrived before demand did. That is not a failure of nerve. It is a sequencing problem. In July 2026, African demand rose 4.7 percent while capacity fell 7.1 percent. One month later, capacity surged 14 percent while demand rose only 3 percent. The two lines are swinging against each other before they have settled into the trade growth that investors are anticipating.</p>
<p>There is a rational reason to build ahead. A trade tracker found Sub-Saharan Africa led all world regions in the first half of 2025, with a 10 percent year on year rise in trade value in current US dollars. The same tracker forecasts trade volume will grow an average of 4.3 percent a year from 2025 to 2029, the second fastest rate globally behind South and Central Asia.</p>
<p>Intra African trade reached 213.8 billion dollars in 2025, up 5.47 percent on 2024. It is forecast to reach 230 billion dollars in 2026, a rise of about 10 percent. The connected market is 54 economies and about 1.5 billion people. That is a substantial demand base, but it does not turn a new aircraft into a full aircraft overnight.</p>
<p>The demand drivers are visible. Operators point to second tier cities in Nigeria, Kenya and Tanzania, foreign investment in life sciences and new energy, and growing e commerce. An express operator is giving small businesses trade training and customs expertise. Those are credible sources of future freight. They are not substitutes for predictable clearance today.</p>

<h2>The bill is behind the border</h2>
<p>About 60 percent of Africa&#8217;s trade costs are unilateral or behind the border. The bill includes customs delays, inefficient logistics, transport restrictions, fragmented standards, services barriers and weak infrastructure. More aircraft can move goods between gateways, but they cannot by themselves make an inspection finish on time, make two standards agree, or make a temperature sensitive shipment safe through an uncertain handoff.</p>
<p>That is why the small exporter matters. A business may have enough orders to fill a container yet still be unable to predict when its own goods will clear. It can plan production, find a buyer and pay for uplift, then face a process whose duration is unclear. The commercial risk is not only the price of transport. It is the inventory, working capital and customer confidence tied up while paperwork moves.</p>
<p>For that exporter, the fix is unglamorous. It is a border process that can be understood and measured, standards that do not change at each crossing, cold chain reliability that survives the journey, and transport rules that do not turn a regional sale into a special exception. Deeper liberalisation of transport, telecoms, financial and professional services could raise intra African trade in services by about 60 to 64 percent by 2035. The opportunity is large because the friction is large.</p>
<p>The bet, then, is rational. New capacity can meet demand from a growing market, and gateways can extend access to second tier cities. But the timing is the problem. Capacity has arrived while the cost of crossing borders remains embedded in every shipment. The operators who win will be the ones that priced the paperwork, not the pallets.</p>

<p>The payoff is not a guaranteed boom, and the load factor will not quickly stop being the worst in the world. If border time becomes shorter and more predictable, freight can reach aircraft when it is ready, and aircraft can carry freight when customers need it. The 36.5 percent figure can improve because the system around the hold works better, not because another hold was added.</p>
<p>So measure the right thing. Track border time, clearance variation, standards compliance and cold chain reliability alongside tonnes and aircraft count. Ask how long a shipment waits after it reaches the gateway, not only how much capacity has been ordered. Africa&#8217;s cargo bet will be won when the paperwork becomes as schedulable as the flight.</p>
