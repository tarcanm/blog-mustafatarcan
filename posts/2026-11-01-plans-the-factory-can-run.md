<p>A factory can meet its weekly volume and still miss an urgent customer order. The apparent contradiction appears when aggregate hours look sufficient, while the required products cannot pass through the line in the necessary sequence. The factory scenario in this article is illustrative, not an actual case. It shows why commercial planning and production execution need a closer working relationship.</p>
<p>Consider a generic food factory making two product families with different allergen cleaning requirements. Material availability limits which orders can start, while qualified staff limit which line or shift can run particular operations. The line therefore has an eligibility problem as well as a capacity problem. A plan may fit the available hours and still describe a sequence the factory cannot safely execute.</p>

<p>Industrial planning connects aggregate choices with executable schedules. At the aggregate level, business leaders weigh demand, margin, customer commitments, supply choices, and network implications. At the detailed level, planners and supervisors turn those choices into feasible operations. Execution then reveals what happened and provides feedback for the next commitment. A production commitment needs a feasible sequence and feedback from the line.</p>
<h2>From intent to sequence</h2>
<p>IBP does not inherently fail at this boundary. It aligns commercial and financial intent with supply choices, and constrained supply planning can include modeled limitations. It can also pass plant and quantity decisions into detailed production sequencing, with information moving back in both directions. The difficulty begins when an aggregate decision is treated as a finished schedule.</p>
<p>A detailed schedule must account for resource calendars, eligible routings, realistic production rates, maintenance, tooling, workforce qualifications, material release, and the order in which operations can occur. It must also represent sequence dependent changeovers, including cleaning requirements that make some product transitions costly or unsuitable. These are operating conditions, not decorative details added after the business plan is approved.</p>
<p>In the illustrative factory, the planner and supervisor compare two imperfect choices. They may keep the current campaign and protect a shorter changeover, or break the sequence to address the urgent order. The second option may require a full allergen clean, a different qualified operator, and overtime approval. The decision also depends on released materials and whether the promised customer date can still be protected. Allergen cleaning must never be shortened or compromised to make the schedule appear feasible.</p>
<p>The supervisor brings knowledge of the line&#8217;s physical burden. The planner brings the customer promise, supply alternatives, and consequences for later orders. Together, they decide whether the exception belongs inside the feasible schedule or needs a business decision. That conversation connects aggregate choices with executable work without treating either role as sufficient on its own.</p>

<h2>The handoff needs ownership</h2>
<p>Good integration starts with a clear handoff contract. It should specify resource calendars, eligible routings, realistic rates, the changeover matrix, and materials that are genuinely released. It should identify which assumptions the detailed planner may adjust and which changes require a return to the business plan. Without that agreement, each team quietly compensates for missing information.</p>
<p>The contract also needs a shared exception review with named decision ownership, escalation thresholds, and authority to approve overtime, sequence disruption, or a customer commitment change. A frozen or restricted horizon protects work already committed, while a more flexible horizon allows the schedule to respond to meaningful constraints. Constant freedom to reschedule can create a new burden for the production team and weaken accountability.</p>
<p>Some decisions belong upstream. If a choice changes service, margin, sourcing, or network design, it should return to IBP or the relevant business planning forum. If it concerns the order of already approved operations within known capacity, it belongs in detailed finite scheduling. Keeping those roles distinct prevents the schedule from becoming a silent place where commercial tradeoffs are made without commercial ownership.</p>
<p>In the illustrative scenario, the revised schedule may preserve the urgent customer order while placing the cleaning activity at a deliberate point in the campaign. The factory then runs the sequence, records actual completion, downtime, quality release, and cleaning time consumed. Those values should inform the next plan. They should not trigger a complete reschedule whenever a transient signal appears during a shift.</p>
<h2>Feedback for the next commitment</h2>
<p>Execution feedback is useful when it changes a planning assumption. Actual completion can show that a rate was optimistic, a resource was unavailable longer than expected, or a changeover matrix needs refinement. Actual cleaning time can alter the next feasible sequence. Quality release timing can affect when material becomes available for the following operation.</p>

<p>The business plan should receive consequences that matter to business choices. A persistent capacity loss, repeated service risk, or expensive changeover pattern may justify a different campaign policy, sourcing decision, or network response. A brief stoppage that does not change the committed plan may remain an execution issue. The integration should distinguish structural deviations from ordinary operating variation.</p>
<p>Teams can propose measures such as schedule adherence, customer service, changeover burden, and frequency of replanning. These are decision aids, not proof of success by themselves. Adherence alone cannot establish customer service or show that inventory is appropriate. Measures become useful when planners and supervisors examine what caused movement, which assumption changed, and who can act on it.</p>
<p>The next committed production plan should pass a specific test before release: sit with the supervisor and trace the customer promise through the actual line sequence. Check eligible resources, released materials, qualified staff, campaign logic, cleaning time, and the escalation path for exceptions. If the supervisor cannot explain how the line will run it, the production commitment requires further work before release.</p>
