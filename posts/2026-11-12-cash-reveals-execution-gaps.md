<p>A manufacturer reports healthy sales and margins while its planner faces competing demands: reduce stock and protect service. Working capital reveals execution problems that sales and margins can hide. An illustrative review follows the operating decisions behind cash committed in inventory, receivables, and payables, distinguishing deliberate choices from friction that requires action.</p>
<h2>Inventory decisions reveal upstream friction</h2>
<p>Inventory is the first place to investigate because it records several earlier decisions. For a manufacturer, the balance includes raw material, work in progress, and finished goods. Each category points to a different question. Raw material may reflect purchasing against an unreliable forecast. Work in progress may signal a constrained step, a changeover problem, or a release plan that outruns production capacity. Finished goods may indicate that production followed a plan that demand, mix, or customer timing did not support.</p>

<p>A local metric can look healthy while the system carries too much cash. A production team may protect utilization by making long runs, and procurement may secure favorable terms by buying larger quantities. Meanwhile, sales may be waiting for a different configuration. The result can be shortage in one location or product family alongside excess elsewhere. The balance sheet does not identify the cause by itself. It tells leaders where the operating story needs to be reconciled.</p>
<p>High inventory is not automatically failure. A deliberate resilience buffer can protect a critical component, absorb supplier variability, or support a seasonal launch. Record the reason, owner, expected duration, and service purpose of that buffer. Review should separate policy stock from inventory created by forecast error, purchase minimums, production sequencing, or blocked orders. The fix may be a forecast handoff, a lot size decision, a capacity constraint, or a customer commitment review.</p>
<p>Inventory days estimate how long stock takes to move to sale. A rising trend deserves investigation across forecast, purchasing, production, and order allocation. Compare consistent periods and relevant peers or business models. Do not impose a universal benchmark. Growth, price changes, seasonality, and a planned buffer can all raise the balance without proving operational failure.</p>
<h2>Receivables test the order to cash path</h2>
<p>Shipment is not collection. Receivables days estimate how long customers take to pay, but the trend needs an operational explanation. Customer terms can support sales while delaying cash. An overdue balance may reflect accepted credit risk, a genuine dispute, missing evidence of delivery, a billing error, or an approval failure. Treating every overdue invoice as a collections problem can conceal a process defect after the goods have left the site.</p>

<p>In the illustrative scenario, finance finds an invoice under dispute after the planner has been told to cut stock. The issue is not resolved by reducing another purchase. The investigation traces the invoice to a shipment with a commercial change that was not reflected in the billing record. Inventory had been built to support the order, service activity had occurred, and collection was delayed by an administrative mismatch. Connect order changes, shipment evidence, pricing, billing, and dispute ownership so the handoff does not create the delay.</p>
<p>Leaders should review receivables by cause and originating decision. Separate normal terms from invoices blocked by missing master data, incomplete documentation, pricing differences, or customer claims. Give each recurring cause an owner in commercial operations, customer service, logistics, finance, or master data. A collection call may recover one invoice. Correcting the handoff can prevent the same cash delay across future orders.</p>
<p>Use the balance sheet as a prompt, not a verdict. A snapshot can be distorted by month end timing, rapid growth, seasonality, or a large shipment before the reporting date. Ratios require flow data and consistent average balances. The useful signal is a persistent or unexplained trend, checked against order volume, shipment timing, terms, disputes, and service performance.</p>
<h2>Connect the cycle to accountable action</h2>
<p>The cash conversion cycle joins the operating sequence. It equals inventory days plus receivables days minus payables days. Inventory days measure the time goods are held, whether or not suppliers have already been paid. Payables days measure the actual average time taken to pay suppliers, not the time contractually allowed. Review agreed payment terms separately from observed payment behavior. A longer cycle ties up more resources. In the indirect cash flow reconciliation, increases in inventory and receivables reduce operating cash, while increases in payables work in the opposite direction. These directional relationships assume other factors remain equal.</p>

<p>Payables therefore require the same care as inventory and receivables. Delaying payment may improve a short term cash view while losing discounts, damaging a supplier relationship, or prompting cash on delivery terms. A supplier payment delay can also weaken the source of a component that production considers critical. Review payment performance against agreed terms, supplier dependency, quality, continuity risk, and the value of early payment. The key question is whether the policy supports the business model.</p>
<p>Bring procurement, planning, production, sales, customer service, logistics, and finance into one review with a shared transaction trail. Start with one slow moving product and one overdue invoice. Trace each to the originating decision, the handoff that failed or worked as intended, and the owner who can change the process. Then agree a conditional improvement, such as a forecast release rule, a billing validation step, or a dispute escalation path. Recheck service and cash outcomes together. Close the review only when an owner, the reason cash is committed, the service outcome, and the next check are recorded.</p>
