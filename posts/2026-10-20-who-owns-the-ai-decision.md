<p>Illustrative scene, not a real company case: a late inbound component arrives with too little stock to cover every order. AI suggests reallocating the limited supply and changing the production sequence, but the planner cannot authorize either move. Procurement protects unit cost, the plant manager protects utilization, sales protects customer promises, and finance protects working capital. Each concern is reasonable. Nobody owns the tradeoff, so a usable recommendation stalls.</p>
<p>That is not mainly a model problem. It is an authority problem. AI changes little until people gain the authority to act on its recommendations. A recommendation becomes operational only when someone can accept it, explain the boundary, trigger the next handoff, and be accountable for the result.</p>

<p>The practical question is therefore not who receives the alert. It is who can convert a conflicted signal into a controlled decision. This proposed operating model puts that question in writing, attaches it to management incentives, and tests it in one real workflow before anyone expands the program.</p>
<h2>Give People the Right to Decide</h2>
<p>Start with one workflow: resolving a material shortage from the first signal to a confirmed customer promise. Name one human cross functional workflow owner, supported by an executive sponsor, with authority to coordinate the tradeoff. The owner is not automatically the planner, but the planner is often the closest person to the exception and should be able to approve within agreed cost, service, and inventory guardrails.</p>
<p>Write four verbs beside each decision: recommend, decide, execute, and consult. The system can recommend a reallocation. The workflow owner decides whether it fits the guardrails. Procurement verifies supplier commitments, production confirms capacity, sales owns customer communication, and the responsible team executes the approved change. Finance pre agrees the monetary and working capital limits rather than appearing only after the decision is urgent.</p>
<p>These are proposals, not universal truths. A business may place the authority elsewhere, but it should not leave the authority undefined. IT supports data quality, model behavior, access, and traceability. IT does not own the commercial or operational tradeoff simply because the recommendation arrived through a technical system.</p>
<p>Guardrails also need a clock. If the decision exceeds them, or if functions disagree about a material risk, a named senior tie breaker resolves the conflict by a finite deadline. That person should receive the facts, the competing consequences, and the recommendation, not an endless chain of vetoes. A decision that expires without an owner is still a decision, usually made by delay.</p>

<h2>Change What Managers Reward</h2>
<p>Authority will remain theoretical if managers punish every local sacrifice that improves the shared outcome. A procurement manager should not be made to look careless merely because an approved shortage response costs more per unit. A plant manager should not be judged solely on utilization when a sequence change protects an important commitment. The scorecard must make the tradeoff visible.</p>
<p>Pair shared customer commitment performance and total cost with functional diagnostics. Diagnostics still matter because they reveal where a process is leaking value, but they should explain the outcome rather than replace it. Do not reward AI acceptance, agreement with the recommendation, or a high automation rate. Those measures invite compliance instead of judgment.</p>
<p>Evaluate the reasoning recorded at the time and review the decision afterward. An accepted recommendation can be wrong, and an override can be correct. The review should ask whether the owner understood uncertainty, constraints, alternatives, and tradeoffs, then whether the approved action was executed and communicated. The point is learning quality, not retrospective punishment.</p>
<p>Managers should coach this judgment rather than become a signoff gate. Protect training time around historic shortage cases, including cases where data was incomplete or the suggested action conflicted with a customer promise. Ask people to explain what they know, what they do not know, and which constraint matters most. Rehearse escalation and handoffs. Prompt syntax is a minor skill beside decision clarity.</p>

<h2>Run One Bounded Trial</h2>
<p>The first trial needs a written one page decision charter for the chosen workflow. Name the owner, executive sponsor, participating functions, authority limits, decision deadline, escalation path, tie breaker, and required closure evidence. State clearly that people may challenge an unreliable suggestion without being treated as resistant to change.</p>
<p>Begin with shadow practice. The owner follows the recommendation, checks the relevant constraints, and records the decision without changing the live process. Then move to bounded live practice using existing approvals, inventory controls, and customer communication rules. The trial should change who can coordinate and decide, not remove safeguards that protect the business.</p>
<p>Close every exception with a short record: the recommendation, the human decision, the reason, the confirmed execution, and the customer communication where relevant. Review unresolved ownership and incentive conflicts, not just model accuracy. A technically accurate recommendation still creates little value if nobody can authorize it or if the handoff fails.</p>
<p>Return to the illustrative planner. Under this charter, the planner could approve a reallocation inside the agreed limits, obtain procurement and production confirmation through defined handoffs, and ask sales to communicate the resulting promise. If the move exceeded a guardrail, the planner would escalate to the named tie breaker before the deadline. The scene changes conditionally, not because AI became infallible, but because responsibility became visible.</p>
<p>On Monday, take one recent planning exception and trace the moment where human authority stopped. Identify the competing objectives, name who should decide, set the guardrails and deadline, and write the escalation path before buying another tool.</p>
