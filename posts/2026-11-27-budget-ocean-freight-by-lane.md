<p>The global container index fell 3%. The Shanghai to Genoa spot rate rose 8%. Both numbers came out in the same week, and only one would have helped an importer trying to book a sailing to Genoa.</p>
<p>On November 26, 2026, Drewry&#8217;s World Container Index dropped to $4,032 per standard container of about 12 meters. Shanghai to Genoa climbed to $4,802, while Shanghai to Rotterdam was flat for the second week at $3,485. The two lanes shared an origin, but the spot benchmarks were $1,317 apart. Falling transpacific rates helped pull down the composite, even as importers trying to book space to Italy faced higher spot rates on their lane.</p>
<p>Build the budget around the lanes you actually book, then roll it up for finance. The order matters. Once the assumptions have been blended into a single rate, it is hard to tell whether the budget moved because freight got more expensive or because more containers moved on the expensive lane.</p>

<h2>Start with the bookings, not the index</h2>
<p>Use one row for each origin, destination, equipment type and planned sailing week. If two services on the same lane have different tariffs or reliability, give them separate rows where the difference affects a decision. A direct service and a routing that depends on a tight transshipment connection may both arrive at the same port, but a buyer with a firm delivery deadline cannot always treat them as interchangeable.</p>
<p>Split the forecast between contract volume you expect the carrier to accept at the agreed terms and the volume you will actually need to buy on the spot market. Check weekly allocations rather than assuming that a quarterly commitment guarantees space in the week you need it. For the contract portion, use the applicable tariff and scope. For the spot portion, start with comparable, bookable quotes and use an index to challenge the direction of travel, not to fill the purchase order.</p>
<p>Set the cost boundary before comparing rates. If finance wants port-to-port ocean freight, keep inland haulage in a separate line. Ask each provider which fuel, terminal and other charges are included. Record the currency, equipment size, route, service and quote expiry. A cheaper quote that excludes a charge the other carrier has included might not be cheaper at all.</p>

<h2>Budget the surcharge where it applies</h2>
<p>Hapag-Lloyd announced a December 1 tariffing date for a peak season surcharge on Far East sailings to North Europe and the Mediterranean. It quoted $500 per TEU for North Europe and $750 per TEU for the Mediterranean. A standard container of about 12 meters counts as two TEU, so those charges work out to $1,000 and $1,500 respectively. Those figures belong on the affected tariff rows, not on every container leaving Asia.</p>
<p>An announcement is a reason to check your quote. It is not proof that every shipment will pay the published amount. Ask which date governs the charge and what happens if the container rolls to another sailing. Confirm whether the charge applies to your contract and whether the spot quote already includes it. Adding it to a benchmark or an all-in quote without checking is an easy way to count it twice.</p>
<p>Consider an illustrative importer planning 100 Rotterdam containers and 50 Genoa containers. Rotterdam has 80 at a contract rate of $3,100 and 20 at a spot budget of $3,500. Ten of those spot boxes are exposed to an additional $1,000 charge, taking the lane budget to $328,000. Genoa has 20 at $4,000 under contract and 30 at a spot budget of $4,800. Twenty-five of those spot boxes are exposed to an additional $1,500, taking that lane to $261,500. The total is $589,500. The rates and volumes in this example are assumptions, not carrier offers. The point is that Genoa makes up one third of the boxes but accounts for roughly 44% of the budget. That is where the next conversation with procurement should start.</p>

<h2>Give each lane a decision rule</h2>
<p>Keep a base case built from confirmed terms and executable quotes, then price a plausible stress case for each exposed lane. For example, if ten Genoa boxes move out of contract allocation and the only usable spot option costs $6,300 including the surcharge, the incremental cost is $23,000 against the assumed $4,000 contract price. Keep inventory risk separate from the freight total, but put the expected delivery date next to both options. A cheaper sailing that arrives after a promotion starts may be the wrong decision.</p>
<p>Agree in advance who can commit space and what would trigger that decision. On a critical lane, the trigger might be the last sailing that protects the receiving date and a backup quote that expires before the next review. On a flexible lane, the buyer may be able to wait. When the month closes, split the variance into changes in total volume, lane mix, rate and surcharges so the next forecast starts with something more useful than last month&#8217;s average.</p>
<p>Before approving the next budget, pick the lane with the largest uncommitted spend. Ask for one executable quote, the surcharge treatment in writing and the last sailing that still meets the receiving date. If those answers change the booking decision, they belong in the budget.</p>
