<p>When a critical mineral becomes constrained, a contract needs to tell the direct supplier what to do and show the buyer what evidence to expect. A useful agreement is rarely a promise of uninterrupted supply. It sets defined duties that procurement, planning and engineering can execute before a production stoppage.</p>
<h2>Disclosure that can be checked</h2>
<p>Start with dependencies that can stop a product. For each relevant part or process, require the direct supplier to disclose the mineral, processing origin where known, affected facility, dependency on a particular grade or form, and point at which visibility becomes uncertain. A Tier 1 supplier should report mapping breakpoints honestly rather than certify complete visibility because a form demands it.</p>

<p>The distinction matters because concentration can increase even when a buyer has several commercial sources. The <a href="https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/executive-summary" target="_blank" rel="noopener">IEA’s Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025</a> reports that, for copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite and rare earth elements, the average share of the top three refining nations rose from around 82 percent in 2020 to 86 percent in 2024. This scoped finding is about refining concentration across those six minerals, not one country’s share and not every critical mineral. It is evidence for asking better questions, not proof that a particular supplier will fail.</p>
<p>Disclosure also needs a test. Name the records that support an assertion, confidentiality boundaries, update events and recipients. A change in processing site, export restriction, qualification status, ownership, or expected delivery should trigger notice to procurement and planning, with engineering included when a substitute may be needed. Audit rights should be proportionate to production risk. A duty that cannot be checked is mainly a statement of intent.</p>
<h2>Allocation that survives scrutiny</h2>
<p>Allocation language often fails because “priority” has no denominator. A workable proposal identifies available capacity, measurement period, products or orders in scope, the method for calculating each customer’s share, and information used to update it. It should explain how the supplier will communicate a revised quota, not imply that the same inventory can be promised to every buyer.</p>
<p>Reserved capacity can be commercially meaningful when it is documented, physically plausible and priced into the relationship. It is not the same as a guaranteed head of the queue. The supplier may lack material, processing access, transport, or lawful permission to export it. No contract can create physical supply or grant legal export permission. A careful agreement therefore commits the supplier to allocation work, records, notices and good faith execution within its actual control, while preserving a candid account of what remains unavailable.</p>

<p>Law may shape that account, but commercial proposals should not be presented as universal legal rules. Maine offers a useful state specific example. Under <a href="https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/11/title11sec2-615.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Maine Title 11, section 2-615</a>, qualifying impracticability affecting only part of a seller’s capacity calls for a fair and reasonable allocation, and the provision addresses seasonable notice and an estimated allocation quota. The conditions, assumed obligations, governing law and other applicable rules matter. Counsel should assess the relevant jurisdiction, export controls, enforceability and force majeure language before anyone treats a draft mechanism as a legal entitlement.</p>
<p>An allocation clause should name the escalation path. Procurement confirms orders, planning models the quota, engineering assesses alternatives and counsel reviews notices and restrictions. The test reveals whether the supplier has people, data and decision rights capable of carrying out the promised response.</p>
<h2>Continuity someone can execute</h2>
<p>Continuity provisions become concrete when they answer questions about stock: does it exist, where is it, who owns it, who funds it, and who can release it? The agreement should identify any buffer, release conditions, replenishment responsibility and access to records.</p>
<p>An alternative is not an available part until engineering approves it. A continuity duty can require the supplier to identify plausible substitutes, provide data, support qualification and notify the buyer of specification changes. It cannot waive safety review or create capacity at a qualified processor.</p>

<p>Consider a hypothetical in which a buyer learns that a refining site change may reduce a motor component’s monthly input. Under a vague contract, the planner waits for a missed delivery. Under a clearer agreement, the supplier sends the defined trigger notice, supplies the current allocation denominator, identifies stock location and release conditions, and reports where its mapping stops. Procurement can compare the quota with the build plan, engineering can examine an approved alternative, and counsel can check export and notice constraints. The information improves the decision window without assuring that production will continue.</p>
<p>These duties cost effort and money. Mapping, audit access, tested notices, stock custody, qualification work and allocation updates require staff, systems and sometimes payment. A buyer should decide which services are worth purchasing and how their cost is shared. A supplier should avoid obligations its data, capacity or authority cannot support.</p>
<p>Before renewal, run one real critical product agreement through a joint test. Choose a plausible shortage trigger, identify the notice recipient, calculate the allocation denominator, locate the supporting records and stock, confirm who may release it, and verify which alternative is qualified. If the contract and its records cannot support that exercise, revise the mechanism before relying on it.</p>
