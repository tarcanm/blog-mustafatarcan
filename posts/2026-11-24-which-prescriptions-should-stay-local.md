<p>Imagine two prescriptions for the same medicine landing at the same pharmacy on a Friday afternoon. One patient has enough until Tuesday and has arranged a Saturday pickup. The other is expecting to pick it up before closing. In the inventory system, both orders call for the same item. On the dispatch board, they belong in different queues.</p>
<p>Walgreens&#8217; new facility in Allentown, Pennsylvania, shows why that distinction matters. The site covers about 6,300 square meters and automates routine parts of prescription filling. It celebrated its opening on November 5, 2026. Walgreens expects it to process more than 28 million prescriptions a year and support nearly 650 stores across six states. That&#8217;s projected throughput, not a measure of how often a patient can collect a prescription on time. <strong>Route prescriptions by when patients need them, not just by what is in stock.</strong></p>

<h2>Work backward from pickup</h2>
<p>A routine refill requested several days ahead is a good candidate for a hub, provided the order is eligible under pharmacy rules and the store can keep the pickup promise. Central filling can take repetitive work out of the store. Walgreens says that is meant to give local teams more time for work with patients. A prescription that needs to be ready today, or one whose details still need review, calls for a different decision. Keep it with the local pharmacy team, which can resolve the exception and choose a feasible fill option.</p>
<p>Start with the promised pickup time and work backward. Leave room for the store to receive the tote, check in the order and make it available. Before that, allow for the delivery route, the hub&#8217;s release and dispatch cutoff, and the time it takes to fill and check the order. If the afternoon truck has already closed, the hub can be fast at filling and still be too slow for this patient. Build the routing rule around what happens on a busy Friday, not what the average order did last month.</p>
<p>Make the deadline explicit on the order. If pickup is booked for Saturday morning, a Monday delivery is a miss even if it meets the hub&#8217;s internal service target. Measure the share of prescriptions ready by the promised time and track the ones that missed it by store, departure window and reason. A network average can hide the stores that consistently lose a day because they sit on the last leg of a route.</p>

<h2>Keep the stock the store will actually need</h2>
<p>Pooling stock at the hub can reduce the need for every store to carry its own supply for slower, predictable demand. That argument breaks down when the store needs inventory before the next delivery. Build the local stock list around the prescriptions the store is expected to cover on short notice. For each item, compare available stock with likely demand until the next reliable replenishment, including the orders already promised locally. Review whether the buffer is protecting pickup times or just expiring on a shelf.</p>
<p>Give the dispatcher a usable inventory number. A pack already reserved for another patient is not available. Neither is the tote listed as in transit when a pharmacist needs the medicine on the shelf. If the store cannot cover an exception, give the pharmacy team a way to confirm stock and timing at an eligible alternate location before making a new promise. Track how often stores have to borrow stock or reroute an order that the system initially treated as covered. Those are early signs that the inventory rule needs attention, even if the patient still gets the medicine on time.</p>

<h2>Rehearse a missed delivery</h2>
<p>Go back to Friday. Suppose the last hub delivery is cancelled and the tote contains the refill promised for Saturday morning. The store needs to know which orders were in that tote and which can still be filled locally. If some orders can move to another hub, that hub needs spare capacity, the right stock and a delivery that reaches the stores in time. Name the person who owns the reroute, and make sure the original order is accounted for before releasing a replacement. The pharmacist needs a reliable update to give the patient while there is still time to change plans.</p>
<p>Run that exercise with a recent day&#8217;s orders, actual pickup promises and the stock and staffing that each store really had. Measure how many patients would miss pickup, how long rerouting takes and which stores would run out of local coverage first. A short exercise with one delivery route will tell you more about the backup than a slide that says the stores will absorb the work. If the backup fails at a particular store, change its routing window, stock or staffing assumptions before moving more volume to the hub.</p>
<p>In the opening example, the two prescriptions may come from the same shelf on a normal day. When the truck doesn&#8217;t arrive, the decision you made about which one could wait determines whether someone makes a wasted trip. Before centralizing the next batch of refills, pick one store and simulate one missed delivery. Start with the patients who already have a time to collect.</p>
