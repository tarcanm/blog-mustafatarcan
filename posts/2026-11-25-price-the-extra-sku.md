<p>In an illustrative sportswear planning meeting, a slow-selling green running shirt is first on the cut list. The planner pulls up the order history and sees it was out of stock through much of the launch. Then she checks a second color, which looks healthier because it sold more. That one sits on a shelf full of unsold stock, and its next run would interrupt production of the black shirt that stores are already chasing.</p>
<p><strong>Keep a SKU only if it adds more value than the best alternative.</strong> What a variant sold last year is one part of that comparison. What customers would buy instead, and what the factory could make instead, are just as useful.</p>

<h2>Give each variant a fair hearing</h2>
<p>Under Armour is aiming to cut its SKU count by 50%. The company reached an earlier 25% reduction goal this year. At the end of its fiscal second quarter of 2027, it reported $1.1 billion of inventory, up 4% year over year. That snapshot does not tell us how much stock the assortment changes will eventually release. It does show why counting fewer items is a poor substitute for looking at the inventory still on hand.</p>
<p>The sportswear example points to a practical problem. A revenue ranking assumes that recorded sales tell us what people wanted. For the green shirt, the planner needs to compare weeks when common sizes were in stock and check whether shoppers bought a different color when their first choice was unavailable. A gap caused by an empty shelf should not carry the same weight as a gap on a fully stocked rack. Conversely, a shirt that sells during deep promotions may have little demand at its regular price.</p>
<p>Start the scorecard at the level where somebody can make a decision: style, color and size by channel, with related items grouped so the team can see what could substitute. Record units sold at full price, net contribution after discounts already taken and variable selling costs, and the item’s role. A core size that completes a retailer’s assortment deserves an explicit review even if it lands near the bottom of the margin ranking.</p>
<h2>Put a price on the next run</h2>
<p>Pull the forecast that was available when the buyer committed, not the latest revision after sales came in. Compare it with sales for comparable weeks when the item was available. Record absolute error in units and the direction of the miss. Persistent overforecasting is a different problem from demand bouncing either side of plan. For a low-volume SKU, an error in units tells the buyer more about the next purchase than a percentage inflated by a tiny denominator.</p>

<p>Next, write down the supplier’s minimum order, usable stock, open purchase orders and the date of the next decision. Estimate likely surplus under a base and a low-demand case. Convert that surplus into expected markdown or write-off exposure, counting each loss once. Show the inventory cash commitment separately. A purchase ties up cash even if the product eventually sells; the full value of that purchase does not belong in the profit calculation as an extra expense.</p>
<p>Ask the factory scheduler for the setup time the variant adds and what that time would actually displace. In a spare shift, three hours of changeover might cost labor and scrap without delaying another order. On a full line, the same three hours could cost a core product its slot. Use the contribution genuinely lost on that slot, without adding the same cost twice. Where two colors share a production run, challenge the assumption that dropping one saves an entire setup.</p>
<h2>Compare keeping it with taking it out</h2>
<p>Suppose the second shirt color in the opening example is expected to contribute $12,000 next season before extra inventory holding costs and markdown risk. Set aside $1,800 for holding costs, $2,200 for incremental markdown exposure, and $2,700 of contribution displaced by its run on a constrained line. Keeping it comes to $5,300. If dropping it would shift enough customers to the core color to earn $8,000, and clearing outstanding commitments would cost $1,500, the exit case is $6,500. The illustrative difference is $1,200 in favor of exiting. None of those figures is Under Armour data.</p>

<p>That recommendation is only as good as the substitution estimate. If the team has no evidence customers will switch, it can stop replenishment in one channel first, using a comparable channel as a check where practical. Watch retained sales, availability of the substitute, aged stock and actual setup hours. If shoppers leave or the core color keeps running out, the test has found a cost the scorecard missed.</p>
<p>At the next assortment review, bring one product family and the next buying deadline. Give each variant an owner and a decision: keep, change how it is supplied, test an exit, or retire. For any proposed cut, write down where the demand should go and which production slot it should free. Then check both at the next review, before using the same logic to cut another family.</p>
